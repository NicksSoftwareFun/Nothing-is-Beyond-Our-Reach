# The Long War {#ch-long-war}

::: flavor
The last pages of the mission book are ruled into columns, Vance's hand, a different ink for every week: *Wells cleaned: 2. Funerals: 1 — Aldridge, the pneumonia, not us. Apples up from the Tolley orchard for the school. Two new checkpoints on Route 9; they are counting again. Dogs at the substation now, the real kind.* And at the very back, one page kept apart, block letters at its head: *HELD IN OUR REACH — the names go here whole. First name, last name, how, and where they lie. The Authority keeps no page like this one. That is the difference, and don't let anybody tell you different.*
:::

A mission is an hour. The war is a season. This chapter owns the season: the roster your crew is drawn from, the three tracks that measure how the war is going, the network of neighbors that makes any of it possible, the sequence you run between missions, and the choices that no dice can make for you. Run it exactly as written. The campaign layer has fewer rolls than a firefight and higher stakes than any of them, because everything here persists — wounds, reputations, arrests, and the names on the back page.

## The Campaign Loop {#sec-campaign-loop}

The campaign is a loop: play a mission ({{ref:ch-running-missions}}), score its aftermath ({{ref:sec-aftermath}}), then run the **between-mission sequence** ({{ref:sec-between-missions}}) — one pass of that sequence is a **campaign turn**, a week or two of fictional time. The sequence ends by selecting the next mission, and the loop closes.

Not every campaign turn holds a mission. A **quiet turn** — chosen with the Lie low action, forced by an empty till, or taken so somebody can teach — runs only steps 3 through 7 of the sequence: nothing was risked, so nothing recovers and nothing is earned, but the valley keeps living, the deck keeps dealing, and the Authority keeps counting. The campaign's calendar is LEAF SEASON's: October into November, and the weather in the scenario sheets worsens whether you use the weeks or waste them.

{{svg:diagrams/diagram-campaign-loop.svg}}

The whole layer lives on two play sheets from the printed set: the **campaign log** — tracks, Supply, the Advance bank, the mission record, the memorial page — and the **network tracker**, one line per asset. Keep them open at every step of this chapter and write in pencil; the campaign is the story of what gets erased. A full between-mission sequence takes twenty minutes at the table. Do not let its speed fool you about its weight: more campaigns are decided in those twenty minutes than in any firefight, because the firefight only ever loses you tonight, and the sequence is where you can lose the winter.

## The Roster {#sec-roster}

The campaign runs on a roster of **8 to 12 named characters** — neighbors, with cards. LEAF SEASON ships with twelve, and their cards are the campaign's whole cast: the printed set carries no replacements beyond what Rapport earns you ({{ref:sec-consequences}}). Support units — drivers, porters, guides, the medics the Auxiliary sends — are not roster: they are one-line stats who come with an asset or a scenario and go home after.

For each mission, pick the lineup: the briefing prints the size, usually five or six. Two constraints always apply. **At most one SOF-vet-tier figure may start any mission** — Deacon is a resource, not a fixture. And nobody who is out on a recovery result may go ({{ref:sec-recover}}).

Lineup selection is the campaign's quiet skill, and it is played against the briefing, not the roster sheet. A raid on a wired compound wants the Lineman and a breacher; a night of pure fieldcraft wants the Stalker and the Scout and nobody who cannot keep quiet at a walk; anything that ends with a truck wants Delgado behind the wheel before it wants a better shooter beside him. Read what the mission will actually spend — Noise, Aid Bag uses, hands for carrying — and staff for the exit as carefully as the objective, because the ford does not care who was brilliant at the transformer. Then look at who you are leaving home, on purpose: the bench is not the leftovers, it is next mission's crew, and the campaign punishes any plan that treats the same five names as the crew and the other seven as scenery.

Plan for rotation, because the recovery tables will impose it. A Serious wound benches a name for a mission or two; a Critical survived may bench them for three and hand back somebody permanently changed. A roster run as six regulars and six spectators collapses the first bad night — the campaign is designed so that your fourth mission is fought by whoever is left standing, which is why Advances exist to spread skills instead of deepening one hero ({{ref:sec-advances}}), and why the Cross-Training rules make teachers of your specialists before the war makes vacancies of them.

**The dead stay dead.** No roll, no late chapter, no mercy. When a character dies, move their card out of the box and write the memorial page: name, mission, how, and where they lie. Whether there was a body to bury is last night's arithmetic ({{ref:sec-aftermath}}); the funeral is a week the living spend ({{ref:sec-network-actions}}).

## The Three Tracks {#sec-tracks}

Three tracks, each running **0 to 10**, are the campaign's instrument panel. They move only when a rule moves them — a scenario's aftermath block, a standing rider ({{ref:sec-aftermath}}), a network action, an event, a Hard Choice. Mark them on the campaign log after every change.

**Rapport — starts at 3.** Your standing with the valley's people and with the G-chief, the old man whose fighters answer to blood and memory before they answer to you. Raised by wins, by restraint, by aid — the deltas print on the sheets, and the Work the valley action buys it directly. Lowered by civilian harm, by failed missions, by bodies and prisoners abandoned. Rapport is the campaign's load-bearing wall: **at 0–1**, the valley closes — no Auxiliary use of any kind, and every Consequences step deals a betrayal ({{ref:sec-consequences}}). **At 8+**, the valley opens — recruits and the safehouse upgrade ({{ref:sec-consequences}}).

**Heat — starts at 2.** The Authority's attention on your corner of the district. Raised by loud wins, ALARM exfils, and captured crew; lowered only by the Lie low action — weeks of nothing, while the Underground spoofs the census rolls and the informers' reports read *quiet*. Heat is the dial the enemy reads: every scenario's garrison line is keyed to your current Heat band, and the Mission Architect budgets OPFOR density and quality from it directly ({{ref:ch-mission-architect}}). **At 8+**, sweep events fire every campaign turn ({{ref:sec-consequences}}), and Exposed assets start disappearing into trucks.

**Capability — starts at 1.** The partner force: how far the G-chief's fighters have come from deer rifles and anger. Raised by the Train action, by training missions, and by captured arms where a sheet says so. Capability is also a currency — fielding a Guerrilla team spends it, and Developing a Guerrilla asset spends it ({{ref:sec-network}}) — so the track breathes: build it, spend it, build it again. A high mark is not a score. It is the possibility that someday this war gets fought by the valley itself.

Read the panel together, not track by track, because the campaign's central tension lives between the first two. Almost everything that raises Rapport fast — big wins, rescues, the fuel that gets a hollow through the cold — raises Heat with it, and almost everything that cools Heat costs weeks the calendar will not give back. The panel's worst reading is not three low numbers; it is high Heat over low Rapport, the Authority leaning on a valley that has stopped covering for you. The best players of this layer treat the tracks the way the crew treats Noise: as a budget spent on purpose, not a score watched with dread.

## The Network {#sec-network}

The Crew is the point of the spear, but the spear is people: the **network**, tracked as assets in three pools on the network tracker. An asset is a person or a place with a name you write yourself — a courier, a barn with a false floor, a clerk in the district office, three cousins with rifles. Each pool unlocks a different kind of help:

- **The Underground** — urban access, intel, forged papers. Underground flips buy the mission's information edge: moved Insertions, revealed Blips, rerolled Contacts, a delayed ALARM ({{ref:sec-insertion}}) — and the Gather intel action between missions ({{ref:sec-network-actions}}).
- **The Auxiliary** — caches, couriers, early warning, safehouses. Auxiliary flips buy logistics and mercy: the extra gear slot, the Exfil extension ({{ref:sec-insertion}}), and off-board medical when a recovery roll turns fatal ({{ref:sec-recover}}).
- **The Guerrillas** — bodies. Flip a Guerrilla asset at setup and spend **1 Capability**: a support team of two to four fighters joins the lineup for one mission, statlines printed by the scenario or built by the Mission Architect. They fight, they carry, and they go home to the hollows after — they are the G-chief's people, not yours, and the dilemma deck will remind you.

Where the network comes from: LEAF SEASON's campaign start sheet prints your opening assets, pool by pool, each with a suggested name you should immediately cross out and replace with a real one — a neighbor, a barn, a route ({{ref:ch-pineland-burning}}); campaigns built from scratch take their starting network from the Mission Architect's budget ({{ref:ch-mission-architect}}). After that, the network grows only one way: the Develop action, one asset at a time, each a week of somebody's carefully spent trust ({{ref:sec-network-actions}}). Write every asset as a line on the network tracker — name, pool, Hidden or Exposed — because the tracker is about to become a casualty list you have opinions about.

**Every asset is Hidden or Exposed.** Using an asset — any flip, any Guerrilla fielding — turns it **Exposed**: somebody moved, somebody talked, somebody's absence was noticed. An Exposed asset cannot be used again until it has rested — flip it back to Hidden with the Rest action ({{ref:sec-network-actions}}), the asset sitting out a mission while its cover heals. Exposed is where the campaign's quietest losses happen: when a Heat sweep fires ({{ref:sec-consequences}}), **every Exposed asset is caught on a d10 roll of 1–4 — lost**: arrested, burned, or gone north. Log lost assets by name. Some of them can be gotten back, and those missions write themselves.

{{svg:diagrams/diagram-network-cells.svg}}

::: designer
The three pools are not invented; they are the architecture of unconventional warfare as it has been taught for seventy years, most famously at the Robin Sage exercise around Camp Mackall — underground for intelligence and access, auxiliary for support, guerrilla force for combat power, cadre to bind them. That course's fictional country is called Pineland, which is a name this book has already tipped its hat to, and Deacon's biography is one long wink at it. We kept the doctrine because it is true: resistance is three different jobs done by three different kinds of neighbor, and the game's economy — flips, exposure, rest — is those jobs' actual risk arithmetic. The twist LEAF SEASON adds is only this: the cadre was not parachuted in. It grew here.
:::

## Between Missions {#sec-between-missions}

After every mission's aftermath, run the between-mission sequence — **seven steps, in this order, no skipping ahead**. On a quiet turn, begin at step 3 ({{ref:sec-campaign-loop}}).

### 1. Recover {#sec-recover}

Every surviving crew member who came home wounded finds their **worst wound** carried off the table and rolls a d20 on the recovery table. The safehouse upgrade adds +2 to the roll ({{ref:sec-consequences}}). Where a line offers an Auxiliary medical asset, the flip happens now, Exposing the asset ({{ref:sec-network}}) — the veterinarian, the requisitioned clinic, the nurse who asks no questions.

{{table:injury-recovery}}

One roll per casualty, for the worst wound only — the lighter ones ride along on the same recovery. A character who comes back, comes back whole: the campaign heals everything the table does not make permanent, and no wound from a previous mission is carried onto the next board. What the table cannot heal it says so — a result that reads "out N missions" means missions actually played: quiet turns do not shorten it. A bench is measured in risks not taken, and a character sits until N missions have been fought without them. A permanent mark rolls on the table below and is written on the card in ink:

{{table:permanent-effects}}

**The lost.** Now resolve everyone the mission's aftermath listed as not out ({{ref:sec-aftermath}}). A figure that was Dead on the board is dead. A Down and Dying figure whose clock was never stopped is dead — nobody came, and the campaign does not pretend otherwise. Anyone lost *alive* — hale, restrained, or stabilized — is **captured**: **Heat +1**, remove them from the roster, and everything they carried is gone. Captured is not dead: log where they were taken. Rescues are missions, built from the raid and infiltration templates ({{ref:ch-mission-architect}}), and sometimes the deck hands you the thread.

**The dead** go to the memorial page, and the page is a rule, not a ritual: name, mission, how, where they lie. Read it before you pick your next lineup. It is the campaign's only scoreboard that never resets.

### 2. Resupply {#sec-resupply}

**Supply** is the campaign's one logistics number: ammunition, diesel, calories, batteries, all abstracted into a single till on the campaign log. It comes in through aftermath loot lines ({{ref:sec-aftermath}}), events, dilemmas, and the caches your Auxiliary builds. It goes out at mission setup ({{ref:sec-setup}}), where every mission presents a bill:

- **The base cost: 1 Supply per mission**, unless the briefing prints a higher number. This is the crew's rifles, food, and fuel for the night.
- **Each item of kit fielded costs its printed Supply.** An Aid Bag is {{stat:gear.med-kit.supply}}; Rifle Plates are {{stat:gear.plates.supply}}; NV Goggles are {{stat:gear.nvg.supply}}. Items priced at 0 cost nothing beyond the base. The cost is per mission fielded — it is the belts, batteries, and wear the item eats, not the item itself, and paying it refills the item's uses and payloads.
- **Crew-served weapons and drones cost more, deliberately.** A crew-served gun fields with its full belt load for **2 Supply**. The drones price themselves on their cards — the Jammer Pack at {{stat:gear.jammer-pack.supply}}, the FPV kit at {{stat:gear.fpv-kit.supply}}, and the FPV's airframe is spent forever when it flies ({{ref:ch-drones}}).

The **gear pool** is everything the crew owns — the printed cards in your box plus what missions have looted. It grows by loot, by scenario award, and by the rare purchase an event puts on the table; it shrinks when things are destroyed, expended, or lost with the people carrying them. During this step, audit the pool: strike what is gone, add what came home, and resolve any purchase the deck has offered. You cannot spend Supply you do not have; a crew that cannot cover next mission's base cost takes a quiet turn and builds the Auxiliary that fixes it.

### 3. Network Actions {#sec-network-actions}

The weeks between missions are worked, not waited out. **Choose two actions** from the table; the same action may be taken twice.

{{table:network-actions}}

Two clarifications. **Lie low** taken at all makes this a quiet turn ({{ref:sec-campaign-loop}}); taken twice, the quiet deepens — Heat −2, still one turn. **Train** and **Work the valley** name people: a teacher, a named crew member with a shovel — characters out on recovery can teach and dig, which is what a bench is for.

### 4. The Event {#sec-event-draw}

The valley moves whether you do or not. The **event deck is 18 cards**; shuffle it at campaign start, discard as drawn, reshuffle when it runs out. Each between-mission sequence, **roll a d20: on 19–20 draw two cards, otherwise draw one**, resolving in draw order. A card marked *Immediately* resolves now; one marked *Next mission* is set beside the log and applied to the next mission's setup. Scenarios and Hard Choices can also instruct draws; they work the same way.

{{card:events.census-sweep}}

That card is the deck in miniature: the Authority's attention arriving as paperwork, a tax paid in exposure, and a choice about which neighbor stands in the doorway holding their papers steady. Events are not weather. They are the war, continuing.

A discarded card is done, but not every card discards on resolution: some events state a condition that keeps them on the table — an informer unfound, a toll unpaid — and those stay face up beside the log, working, until their own text says they stop. When two *Next mission* cards are waiting at once, both apply; where they collide, the Good-Faith Clause reads them against you ({{ref:sec-good-faith}}). And when a card's instruction is impossible — no Auxiliary asset to flip, no cache to lose — do exactly what the card says to do about it, and nothing else; the deck was written knowing your ledger might be empty, and an empty ledger is its own kind of news.

### 5. Consequences {#sec-consequences}

Now read the tracks — after the event, so its deltas count. Three checks, every campaign turn:

- **Heat 8+: a sweep fires.** Roll a d10 for every Exposed asset: on **1–4 it is lost** ({{ref:sec-network}}). The sweep does not lower Heat. The trucks will come again next turn unless you cool it.
- **Rapport 0–1: the valley closes.** No Auxiliary use of any kind — no flips, no support units, no medical — until Rapport climbs to 2. And draw a **Hard Choice** now ({{ref:sec-hard-choices}}): at this Rapport, somebody is already talking, and the deck decides who.
- **Rapport 8+: the valley opens.** Choose one, now: a **recruit** — add a new Green character to the roster, from the scenario book's recruit list, up to the roster cap of 12 — or the **safehouse upgrade**, once per campaign: the network's medicine gets walls and a stove, and every recovery roll takes +2 from now on ({{ref:sec-recover}}).

Run the checks in that order, and note that they are states, not one-time alarms: a track that stays past its threshold fires its consequence every campaign turn until you move it. Heat 8+ is not a storm that passes. It is a season the valley lives in until you buy your way out of it, one quiet week at a time.

### 6. Advances {#sec-advances}

After each mission, the crew banks **2 Advances** — shared points, kept on the campaign log. Spend any number of banked Advances now, on characters who went on the just-completed mission and came back alive; unspent points keep. Every purchase is paid in full, and the costs climb — competence is bought retail:

The ceilings are part of the design. Nobody trains past the 9/11 tier — the 8/10 line is a lifetime, not a course, and the roster's one card that carries it came by it across twenty years. Marksmanship's range day is never free: pay its Supply, and on its printed risk roll of 1 the shooting is reported — treat it as **Heat +1**. Grit is bought only with bad nights, exactly as the card states, which means the crew member who has been through the most is the one who can grow the most: hard lessons are the campaign's honest name for them.

Cross-Training is how the roster survives itself: any skill below can be learned from a teacher who has it — a living, present crew member, or the named network asset — under Cross-Training's printed conditions. Read the teacher lines before you need them: two of these skills die with a single name apiece, and the Leader line cannot be taught at all until the valley trusts you, because command here is given by the led. Skills die with people. Teach early, and teach the skills whose loss you cannot plan around — a second medic and a second driver have saved more campaigns than any marksmanship step ever has.

### 7. The Next Mission {#sec-next-mission}

Close the turn by choosing what comes next. In LEAF SEASON, the arc's own order and branches are printed in {{ref:ch-pineland-burning}}; events and Hard Choices add missions of their own — a lockup with a name in it, a convoy fight in the first snow — and the Mission Architect builds anything else the campaign needs ({{ref:ch-mission-architect}}). Whatever you choose, read it against the panel: the briefing's garrison line is keyed to your current Heat, your lineup is whoever Recover left standing, and the network you just spent is the network you will have. If instead this was a quiet turn, note it and start the next sequence when its week is done.

## Hard Choices {#sec-hard-choices}

Some nights the dice rest and the campaign asks you directly. The **Hard Choices deck is 12 cards**. One is drawn when a scenario instructs it — mid-mission or in its aftermath — when an event or another card instructs it, or when the Consequences step calls one at Rapport 0–1. Drawn blind, shuffle and draw, or roll a d12 on the table version.

Read the card aloud. Choose option A or option B. Apply every printed cost immediately, or on the schedule the card states; roll only where an option prints a roll. There is no option C, and the Good-Faith Clause ({{ref:sec-good-faith}}) has a campaign face: if you have found a reading in which a Hard Choice costs you nothing, you have misread the card.

A card drawn mid-mission by a scenario's script resolves right there, at the table, before the next activation — the game stops because the fiction has: somebody is standing in the orchard waiting for your answer. Its campaign costs land in the aftermath; anything tactical it changes, changes now. However a card arrives, log it — the card, the option, the turn — because several of them keep charging after they resolve, and one or two are waiting to see whether you ever slip. A Hard Choice is never finished the night you make it. That is what makes it one.

{{card:dilemmas.the-prisoner}}

{{card:dilemmas.the-medic}}

Notice what the deck keeps doing: it prices Rapport against everything else you want. Capability wants the prisoner shot; Rapport is the families who will hear about the orchard. Heat wants the medic held; Rapport is the valley learning you sent a nineteen-year-old home. The tracks are honest about this — the valley's good opinion is the currency every other currency eventually converts through, because without the people there is no network, no warning, no ford with a truck at it. There is no correct answer on any card. There is only which cost you can carry, said out loud, in front of the log you will read at the end.

::: designer
Robin Sage grades its candidates on rapport, and it is closer to pass/fail than any marksmanship score: the exercise's guerrilla chief is scripted to demand things — a prisoner shot, a village punished — that the candidate must refuse *without losing him*, because a cadre that wins its arguments and loses its host has failed at the only thing it is for. THE PRISONER is that scripted night, dealt to your table. The deck's job is to make the campaign's real skill the one Robin Sage tests: keeping your people, your conscience, and your war in the same hand — and knowing which one you will put down when a card finally makes you choose.
:::

## How It Ends {#sec-campaign-end}

LEAF SEASON has an ending: six linked scenarios, October into November, and when the last one is scored, {{ref:ch-pineland-burning}} reads your panel — Rapport, Heat, Capability, and the names still on the roster — against the coming winter and tells you what the valley's season bought. First frost is already in the deck; the passes close whether the work is done or not.

Past the arc, the campaign is open-ended, and the Mission Architect will feed it as long as you like ({{ref:ch-mission-architect}}). Open-ended does not mean endless. Endings worth honoring, when they come: the roster can no longer field a lineup, and the valley's war goes quiet for a generation. Rapport reaches 0 and stays — the campaign is over whatever the roster says, because a crew without a valley is just armed men in the woods. Or Capability stands at the top of its track with the tracks holding, and the war stops needing you: the G-chief's people take the night shifts, and the crew's last mission is teaching. The valley endures, or it doesn't. Either way, end the campaign the same way: read the log front to back, memorial page last, out loud if anyone is with you. That page is what the motto cost, and what it reached.

::: example
**The week after the substation.** Mission two ended with the transformer in pieces, a prisoner walked to the county line — THE PRISONER, option B, resolved in the aftermath — and the panel reading Rapport 5, Heat 5, Capability 1, Supply 4. Ledford came home Serious, a leg wound; Vasquez took a Light to the arm; Okafor was Shaken twice and hit once. Now the sequence, in order.

*1 — Recover.* Ledford rolls a d20 for the Serious: **11** — out 1 mission. Sugar Creek's best stalker will miss mission three. Vasquez's Light needs a d20 only to check the sit-out: **9** — back next mission. Nobody was lost; the memorial page stays one name long.

*2 — Resupply.* The till reads 4. The pool audit strikes nothing — the demo kit's spent charges refill whenever it next fields — and no purchase is on offer. Next mission's bill gets penciled: base 1, plus 2 if Boone's gun goes out.

*3 — Network actions.* Two picks. **Develop**: a new Hidden Auxiliary asset — the Tolley barn cache, a false floor and forty gallons. **Rest**: the schoolhouse courier, the Underground asset Exposed before mission two to reveal a Blip, flips back to Hidden.

*4 — The event.* The d20 comes up **9**: one card. **CENSUS SWEEP** — Heat +1, to 6, and one Auxiliary asset must flip Exposed to keep its cover story intact or be lost on a d10 roll of 1–3. The barn cache is three days old and already standing in a doorway holding its papers. Flip it Exposed rather than gamble it.

*5 — Consequences.* Heat 6: under 8, no sweep — but the next briefing's garrison line will read from the Heat 6 row, and it shows. Rapport 5: neither threshold. Nothing fires. The Exposed cache is a held breath until somebody spends a Rest on it.

*6 — Advances.* The crew banks 2, holding none, and spends both on Tanya Okafor: **Hard Lessons**, cost 2, legal because she was wounded at the substation. Her Grit improves from 10 to 9 — and a Grit of 9 or less can Rally ({{ref:sec-rally}}). The lineman who froze in the yard in mission one is becoming somebody people get up for.

*7 — The next mission.* Mission three, without Ledford. The quiet approach just lost its Stalker, so the plan leans on Birdy's Scout and on darkness — and on the fact that Heat 6 means the checkpoints are double-manned now. The sequence is over. Winter is a number of gallons, and the barn is holding forty of them, Exposed.
:::

The war you have just read the rules for is long on purpose. Missions are won inside an hour; the valley is kept or lost across a season of these turns — recover, resupply, work, listen, choose, learn, go again. {{ref:ch-pineland-burning}} deals the first hand.
