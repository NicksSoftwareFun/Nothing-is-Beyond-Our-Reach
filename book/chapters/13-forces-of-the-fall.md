The last chapter was the enemy's skeleton — the scripts every element runs ({{ref:ch-enemy-system}}). This chapter is the flesh: the four powers that field those elements, what their people are like, how their units combine, and every enemy card in the game, faction by faction. Read it as the crew reads its ledger — not to admire the enemy, but to pick, at two hundred meters, which fight you are in.

## Reading an Enemy Card {#sec-reading-opfor-cards}

Enemy cards are deliberately lean — a statline and a script reference, no biography ({{ref:sec-statline}}). Every field on one is a handle into a procedure you already know:

- **Shoot `Open/Cover`** — the two TNs the figure attacks with, chosen by your cover exactly as yours are ({{ref:sec-cover}}, {{ref:sec-attack-procedure}}).
- **Grit** — what the figure rolls against when its group breaks: half the element down, or its Leader dead ({{ref:sec-opfor-morale}}).
- **Armor** — subtracted straight off the Casualty Die when you hit it ({{ref:sec-opfor-casualty}}).
- **Doctrine** — the script the element runs every Warden Phase ({{ref:sec-doctrines}}). *Per element* means the scenario assigns it at setup.
- **Weapon** — a profile from the arsenal ({{ref:sec-arsenal}}); its DR is the Casualty Die's other half when the figure hits you.
- **Special** — the one rule that makes this unit itself. A special overrides the general rule wherever they disagree.
- **Element** — the grouping the card fights in, which is the unit the Warden activates and the unit that tests morale ({{ref:sec-elements}}).

Blips resolve into these cards too: when the Contact Table names a "patrol pair" or a "fireteam" ({{ref:sec-blips}}), the scenario's faction tells you which cards to deal and the card's element line tells you how they stand. That is the whole literacy. Everything below is who carries the cards.

## The Authority {#sec-faction-authority}

**INTSUM.** The Emergency Continuity Authority is a payroll, a fuel ledger, and a chain of command that still believes in itself. Its professional core is real — career soldiers and federal officers who never left their posts — and it fights like a bureaucracy that has read its own manuals: sentry pairs on fixed comms, patrols on schedules, machine guns sited where the map says they should be, and a reporting culture that treats the radio as a weapon system. Its people are adequately trained, adequately armored, and genuinely disciplined; they hold under fire that would scatter anyone else in the valley. Their weakness is structural, not personal. Everything runs on the leader, the RTO, and the schedule — cut those and the remainder is well-drilled men waiting for instructions that are not coming. The Authority does not avenge its dead tonight; it files them, and comes back with more of everything. Assume every fight you start with the ECA is being written down.

**In the field.** The ECA fights as a system of mutually supporting elements: sentry pairs (Holding) screen an objective, an MG team (Base-of-Fire) owns the widest lane, fireteams of four sweep or assault as tasked, and a command element — Officer and RTO — sits on fixed comms and runs the Directive deck harder than anyone ({{ref:sec-directives}}). Unaware, it is all posts and patrol schedules you can time. Suspicious, a single figure walks out to look while the posts stay manned. Alerted, the fireteams maneuver against your Known positions under Directives while the Leaders reach for the net — and ALARM is what the ECA is *for*: an SRT team or mounted QRF on the clock, drones up, the whole board tightening ({{ref:sec-qrf-clock}}). Priorities against them never change: Leaders, RTOs, the AG on the gun. The Rifleman's discipline forgives one failed morale test a mission ({{ref:sec-opfor-morale}}); nothing forgives the element that loses its radio.

The marksman deserves a margin note: his special rewrites target priority ({{ref:sec-target-priority}}) — he is hunting your Leaders and your Gunner past every nearer target, which makes him the one enemy on the board whose priorities mirror yours. The drone operator is not a rifleman at all; he is the Warden's supply line for Known ({{ref:sec-warden-drones}}), and his card says the counter out loud: kill him and the aircraft lands. The SRT team is the elite exception to everything: Assault Doctrine, flashbang entries that take the Entry Fight away from you ({{ref:sec-entry}}), morale at a level that means they mostly do not break — they arrive with ALARM, and they are the argument for making sure ALARM never gets called.

### The Civil Order Corps {#sec-faction-coc}

**INTSUM.** The Corps is the Authority's local auxiliary: neighbors who signed on for rations, requisition shares, or the pleasure of a uniform, led by bosses who were owed favors before the Fracture and collect them at gunpoint now. They are numerous, poorly trained, and unevenly brave — jumpy at first blood, vicious when the odds run their way, and led by men whose cruelty is a management technique. The ECA uses them the way you would expect: checkpoints, cordons, and bodies for the sweeps its professionals are too few to walk. Do not confuse cheap with harmless. A Corps squad is six rifles, it knows your face, and its boss keeps it in the fight well past the moment its nerve is gone.

**In the field.** Corps squads of six fight under a Boss, usually as Cordon or Sweep — the ECA gives them the ground it can afford to lose. Unaware they are lazy posts and shortcut patrols; Suspicious they investigate in a bunch, because nobody wants to walk toward the noise alone; Alerted they are a lot of Snap Shots and a Boss shouting his squad's spine straight; and under ALARM their technicals mount the heaviest gun the faction owns and arrive as cut-rate QRF ({{ref:sec-doctrine-qrf}}) — read the technical's card before you cheer, because an HMG does not care who is holding it ({{ref:sec-hmg}}). Their morale is glass with a reroll behind it — see the worked example below — and the whole faction's hinge is the Boss: while he lives the squad rerolls its failures; drop him and the same squad takes a Leader-death test with no net ({{ref:sec-opfor-morale}}).

::: example
**First blood at the low bridge.** A Corps squad — six Corpsmen and a Boss, Sweep Doctrine — is working the creek line when Ledford's rifle takes the point man off his feet: **Down.** The Corpsman card's Jumpy special triggers on the element's first casualty: an immediate group-morale test, every survivor rolling 2d10 against its own Grit ({{ref:sec-opfor-morale}}). The five Corpsmen need 11: they roll **13, 6, 10, 16, 9** — two hold, three fail. The Boss needs his 10 and rolls **12** — he holds, and his Cruel special lets the Corpsmen reroll their failures once while he lives: the **6 rerolls to 14**, the **9 to 11** — saved — but the **10 rerolls to 8**. One Corpsman falls back a Doctrine step; Sweep is off the ladder, so he drops to Holding and goes firm behind the bridge rail while his squad walks on without him ({{ref:sec-doctrines}}). One rifle shot, one man down, one man quit. Now consider what the same shot does tomorrow, aimed at the Boss.
:::

## The Compact {#sec-faction-compact}

**INTSUM.** The Compact is what county militias become when they federate into a business: protection, tolls, and boundary-keeping, enforced by men who hunted these ridges before any of this and will hunt them after. They are not an army and do not fight like one — no fixed posts to speak of, no radios worth jamming, just bands of four to six who move through timber like they own it, because in every sense that matters they do. The Compact is not looking for your war. It is looking for its cut, its border respected, and its name said carefully. It will trade, it will parley, and it will kill you with patience if you make either impossible. Treat every Compact contact as a negotiation that has not yet picked its language.

**In the field.** Compact bands favor Sweep and Cordon on their own ground — they patrol what they tax and block what they do not — and their Woods-wise special cuts your eyes, not theirs: every Concealment spotting distance against a Compact figure or Blip is halved, so the concealed contact you would resolve at six squares is, when it is Compact, at three before you make it out ({{ref:sec-blips}}, {{ref:sec-spotting}}). Their ladder tops out low. A Compact board is timber and hollows, not radio nets: with no RTOs and no fixed sets, most Compact scenarios can reach ALARM only through the fallback timer — a band overdue at the store — and what ALARM summons is not a QRF column but the rest of the family, entering as Blips wherever the scenario says the ridge paths run. Their Headmen carry the faction's real weapon instead: parley. Where a scenario allows it, a contact becomes an Interact and a contest of Grit instead of a firefight — a door the ECA never offers, on the terms the scenario prints. Their morale is a professional's ({{ref:sec-opfor-morale}}): they break like men with somewhere better to be, and a band that quits tonight has not forgotten you exist.

## The Ferrymen {#sec-faction-ferrymen}

**INTSUM.** The Ferrymen move things: fuel, medicine, ammunition, people, and information, across every line on the district map, for anyone whose payment clears. They are a network, not a territory — crossings, cutouts, and couriers — and their gunhands are exactly what the name says: hired security for cargo, paid to make robbery expensive rather than to win wars. A Ferrymen escort fights briskly, professionally, and only as long as the arithmetic favors the payroll; heroism is not in the contract. Some missions they are your vendor, some missions your target, and the network remembers which you were. Of the valley's four powers they are the only one you can simply *buy* — and the only one whose grudge follows you into the campaign's ledgers rather than onto the board.

**In the field.** Gunhands work in escort pairs, Holding around whatever they are paid to keep — a crossing, a cache, a truck ({{ref:sec-doctrine-holding}}). Their submachine guns are close-work weapons that lose no TN inside Zones, so pry them out of buildings with grenades and angles, not doorways ({{ref:sec-entry}}). Their alert behavior barely climbs the ladder: Unaware they watch the cargo, Suspicious they pull in tighter around it — a Holding element's investigating figure never wanders far from the pay — and Alerted they fight from cover exactly as long as the arithmetic holds. No Ferryman ever calls a QRF that does not exist; when a Ferrymen scenario has an ALARM worth the name, it is usually a signal to move the cargo, not reinforce it, and the mission clock is what tightens. Their card carries their whole soul — paid, not devoted: at half casualties the element routs automatically, no test ({{ref:sec-rout}}). Kill one and his partner is already leaving.

## Marauders {#sec-faction-marauders}

**INTSUM.** Marauders are transient predators — packs of five to eight built around a chief, feeding along the roads and the softest farms, moving on when a place is chewed through. They hold nothing, garrison nothing, and answer to nothing; a marauder contact is weather, not policy. What makes them dangerous is that they do not fight like anyone else in this book: no posts, no comms, no fallback plan — just a shotgun rush that arrives all at once, at close range, screaming. What makes them survivable is the same fact. The pack's whole cohesion is the chief's presence, and its nerve is the worst in the valley; marauders break early, break hard, and do not rally, because nothing but momentum was holding them together. Fear them inside six squares. Bury them with distance, discipline, and one well-chosen bullet.

**In the field.** Marauders run Assault, always, and their Frenzied special turns every Directive into the same order: Sprint at the Known ({{ref:sec-doctrine-assault}}). There is no alert ladder to speak of with a pack on the board — Unaware they are already moving, Alerted they are merely louder, and there is no QRF because there is no one to call. The pack's arithmetic is printed on two cards: group morale at +2 means every test sheds bodies ({{ref:sec-opfor-morale}}), and the Chief's card ends the fight outright — the pack routs when he dies, full stop. Against marauders, target priority is a one-name list.

## Assembling the Enemy {#sec-assembling-elements}

The *element* line on each card is the faction's building block, and scenarios assemble garrisons from those blocks: ECA fireteams of four under a Team Leader, sentry pairs, a two-man gun team, an Officer-and-RTO command element; Corps squads of six under a Boss; Compact bands of four to six under a Headman; Ferrymen escort pairs; marauder packs of five to eight under a Chief. When a card's Doctrine reads *per element*, the scenario assigns the script at setup — the same four riflemen are a Sweep patrol in one mission and an Assault team in another. Leaders matter twice over in that arithmetic: the card that leads an element is also the figure whose death forces its group test ({{ref:sec-opfor-morale}}), which is why every faction's block is written around one man you can name.

Each faction leans on the ladder differently, and you can read a scenario's temperament from its roster. An ECA garrison is Holding and Base-of-Fire wrapped around an objective, Sweep patrols on its clock, and an Assault or QRF fist behind the ALARM it will spend the whole mission trying to call. The Corps mans Cordons and sweeps in numbers, because numbers are what it has. The Compact sweeps and blocks its own borders and would rather talk than test its morale table. Ferrymen hold points and cut losses. Marauders assault, only and always — the one force in the game with nothing worth defending, which is exactly what makes them immune to half your tricks: no leaders to isolate but one, no comms to cut, no post to slip past. Mixed boards happen, and the factions are not allies: a scenario that puts the Corps and the Compact on the same map will say who shoots at whom, and the Warden runs each force on its own ladder as the sheet directs. Building your own garrisons — points, budgets, and the density Heat buys — is the Mission Architect's business ({{ref:ch-mission-architect}}), and the campaign's Heat track decides how much of the Authority's attention your valley has earned ({{ref:ch-long-war}}).

::: designer
No faction in this book has an ideology, and that is load-bearing. The Authority is continuity of government that never gave anything back; the Corps is opportunism in a uniform; the Compact is a toll; the Ferrymen are a price; marauders are appetite. Nobody says left or right, ever — partly so the table stays open to anyone, but mostly because the game is about something more durable than a platform: what occupation *is*, procedurally — checkpoints, censuses, requisitions, informers — and what it costs neighbors to refuse it. The moment a faction stands for a position, the player is invited to argue with the faction instead of the fact. The fact is the checkpoint. The argument is the mission.
:::
