# Welcome to the Fall {#ch-welcome}

::: flavor
From an ECA District 9 stability assessment, October of the fourth year: *"Rural population compliance nominal. Insurgent capability assessed negligible — no heavy weapons, no air support, no secure communications, no professional cadre."* Beneath it, in pencil, in a careful hand: *They counted everything we don't have. Nobody counted the hollows. Nobody counted the deer rifles, or who strings their wire, hauls their fuel, fixes their trucks, and digs their graves — and remembers where all of it lives.* Fresh paint on the Route 9 water tower this morning, letters a yard tall, facing the checkpoint: **NOTHING IS BEYOND OUR REACH.**
:::

## The Fall {#sec-the-fall}

Four years ago the Fracture broke the federal government — a constitutional crisis, not a bomb or a plague. Nobody nuked anybody. The lights went out anyway, east of the mountains, everywhere except the enclaves where the Emergency Continuity Authority kept them on for itself.

The Authority — the ECA — began as continuity of government and became an occupation that never noticed the difference. Its professional core is real: career soldiers and federal officers who stayed at their posts. Around that core it recruited the Civil Order Corps, local collaborator militia, poorly trained and numerous, and above both it fields SRT — special reaction teams, the ones you hope arrive late. The Authority runs checkpoints, census sweeps, drone surveillance, and requisitions. It holds the towns, the fuel, and the airwaves.

It does not hold the hollows. The valley has other neighbors too: the Compact, county militias federated into a protection racket; the Ferrymen, a smuggling network that is sometimes a vendor and sometimes a target; and marauders, transient predators who answer to nothing.

And it has you. The Crew you command are not commandos parachuted into someone else's war. They are the valley's own — veterans, hunters, line workers, drivers, medics, a preacher — doing small, precise, dangerous things in the dark. The motto is theirs, not the Authority's: painted under overpasses, on water towers, on the backs of Authority signs. It is a promise about the valley's people and their reach into every hollow, shop floor, and radio room the Authority thinks it holds. Every mission in this book quietly proves it.

The campaign is called **LEAF SEASON**: the Uwharrie–Blue Ridge corridor of the western Carolinas, October into November. Hunting season puts armed people on the roads with an alibi. The leaves come down scene by scene, and the sightlines get longer. Then first frost, and the passes close.

## What This Game Is {#sec-what-this-is}

NOTHING IS BEYOND OUR REACH is a solo tabletop wargame. One player — you — runs the Crew, figure by figure, on a gridded map. Everything that hunts you is run by **the Warden**: not an app and not an opponent's imagination, but a system of dice, tables, Doctrine scripts, and a deck of Directive cards. The Warden's forces patrol, investigate, flank, radio for help, and break, all by procedure. Your half of the bargain is to run those procedures sharply and honestly ({{ref:sec-good-faith}}).

It is a heavy game. It tracks which way a figure faces, how much noise your crew has made, where the machine-gun fire is beating the ground, and how fast a friend is bleeding. It is also a lethal game: there are no hit points to whittle. A single aimed rifle shot fired well ends a fight more often than not — in both directions.

A mission runs 60 to 120 minutes at the table, typically six to ten game turns. A turn is about thirty seconds of fiction; a square is about a meter and a half; a standard board is 30 by 24 squares. Missions chain into a campaign in which your people carry their wounds, their reputations, and their dead from one to the next.

::: example
**One exchange, whole.** Turn three of a raid. Reyna Vasquez — 82nd Airborne once, the valley's now — is prone at the corner of a stock fence with her carbine, unspotted. An ECA Rifleman breaks from a shed toward a stone wall twelve squares out, well inside the carbine's Effective Range of {{stat:weapons.carbine.eff}} squares, and makes it. The wall is Cover, so Vasquez is shooting against the second of the two numbers printed on her card: Shoot {{stat:crew.vasquez.shoot}}.

She has not moved this activation, and she takes the time for an Aimed Shot. She rolls two d10: a 7 and a 6, total 13. That clears her Cover TN. A hit.

The trooper is an OPFOR figure, so there is no wound bookkeeping — one roll of the Casualty Die. She rolls a d10: a 5, plus the carbine's Damage Rating of {{stat:weapons.carbine.dr}}, minus his Armor of {{stat:opfor.eca-rifleman.armor}}, for a total of 7. Read it off the table: Dead.

Two rolls, one dead trooper — and one unsuppressed gunshot. The shot is Noise, Noise climbs the Alert Ladder, and the fence corner is now a Known position. Somewhere behind that shed a Team Leader is reaching for his radio, and the next ninety seconds are about whether he gets to use it.
:::

::: designer
This game is heavy on purpose. It tracks facing, noise, blood, and battery life because the fantasy on offer is competence under pressure, and competence is made of details. But every subsystem funnels into the same two ten-siders against a target number, and the lethality does the pacing for us: firefights stay short because the math makes them short. Respect for that arithmetic is the game's real difficulty setting.
:::

## What You Need {#sec-what-you-need}

- **This book.** Rules in the front, the enemy system and campaign in the middle, scenarios and reference in the back.
- **Dice.** A standard polyhedral set: two ten-sided dice, one six-sided, one eight-sided, one twenty-sided. Each die type has one fixed job in this game and never does another's; the jobs are laid out in {{ref:sec-dice}}.
- **The printed set** (in the `release/` files, print-ready on US Letter): the **cards** — Crew, gear, OPFOR units, the Warden's Directive deck, events and dilemmas; the **tokens** — crew, OPFOR, Blips, vehicles, drones, and status markers; the **maps** — six scenario boards, tiled for home printing; the **play sheets** — crew roster, campaign log, and network tracker; and the **quick reference**.
- **A pencil.** The campaign log expects to be written on, and so do the margins of your maps.
- **Table space** for a 30-by-24-square board and a card rail beside it.

Nothing else. There is no app, no internet lookup, and nothing to charge.

## How to Read This Book {#sec-how-to-read}

Three conventions carry the whole book. First: **a rule is stated once**, in the chapter that owns it, and everywhere else a cross-reference points at that statement with a page number. If two passages ever seem to disagree, the owning chapter is right. Second: **a capitalized term is a rules term**, defined in the glossary — when you read Shaken, Known, or Noise with a capital letter, it is machinery, not mood. Third: **the fenced boxes have fixed jobs.** Fiction lives only in chapter openers like the one above and in flavor margins. An *example* box is a rule performed with real dice, worth playing along with. A *designer's note* explains why a rule exists. A *sidebar* is optional or advanced material — skip every sidebar on your first read and lose nothing.

The chapters run in learning order. Chapters 2 through 4 are the machine: Core Concepts covers the dice, tests, the map, line of sight, and cover; Actions and the Turn covers action points, the turn sequence, and the Interrupt Die; Fieldcraft covers noise, stealth, and hidden contacts. Chapters 5 through 8 are the fight: Ranged Combat, Close Quarters, Wounds and Recovery, and The Human Factor. Chapters 9 through 11 are the heavy equipment: Crew-Served Weapons, Vehicles, and Drones and Electronic Warfare. Chapters 12 and 13 are the enemy: The Enemy System — the Warden's doctrines and directives in full — and Forces of the Fall. Chapters 14 and 15 cover missions and the campaign: Running Missions and The Long War. Chapter 16 is Pineland Burning, the six linked LEAF SEASON scenarios. Chapter 17, Mission Architect, is the toolkit for building your own. The appendices collect every table, the complete arsenal, and the index.

Read chapters 2 through 4 with the quick reference beside you, skim 5 through 8, and go play. Come back for the heavy-equipment chapters when a scenario puts a machine gun or a drone in your hands. Read chapter 12 when you start wondering why the Warden does what it does — the scenarios run correctly before you understand them.

::: sidebar
**If you have played other wargames,** four habits to unlearn. There are no hit points: a hit on your people rolls location and severity, and one bad number ends a character's war ({{ref:ch-wounds}}). Cover is never a die modifier: every attack prints two target numbers, Open and Cover, and you simply use the right one ({{ref:sec-cover}}). Activating your whole force in one safe block is not free: the Interrupt Die taxes greed ({{ref:sec-interrupt-die}}). And an enemy that has not acted yet is not safe to cross — unspent action points wait as Reaction Fire ({{ref:sec-reaction-fire}}).
:::

## The Spirit of Play {#sec-spirit-of-play}

You hold both jobs at this table: the Crew's commander and the Warden's operator. Keep them separate. When you command the Crew, be cunning, cautious, and unfair. When you operate the Warden, be exact.

Expect to lose things. Missions get scrubbed, crews come home carrying someone, and a withdrawal with everyone alive is a win — the campaign rules in {{ref:ch-long-war}} treat it as one. The dead stay dead; the campaign log keeps a memorial page, and it will not stay empty.

Civilians are present in this world and they are not props. The rules never ask you to be good; they make it expensive to be careless, through the Rapport the valley extends or withdraws ({{ref:ch-long-war}}). Protecting people is not the sentimental choice. It is the sound one.

Last: this game is deliberately ideology-free. Nobody in the valley says left or right, and neither does this book. The Authority is what continuity of government becomes when it never gives anything back. The Crew are the neighbors who decided that was not an ending. That is the whole politics of it.

## The Good-Faith Clause {#sec-good-faith}

This is a rule, not advice. It governs every ruling you make on the Warden's behalf, from your first turn to your last.

When a Doctrine script, a Directive, or any Warden procedure is ambiguous, resolve it in the most tactically sensible way *against* the Crew. When two readings are exactly equal, take the one less favorable to you. Break every tie against the player. **You are the Warden's hands, not its lawyer.** Do not look for the reading that spares your people; look for the one a competent enemy would take, and take it.

The clause is reprinted beside the Doctrine scripts in {{ref:ch-enemy-system}}, where you will use it most.

::: designer
Solo games die of the benefit of the doubt — a generous line-of-sight call here, a sentry who investigates a little slowly there, until the opposition is a scarecrow and winning stops meaning anything. The Good-Faith Clause is the vaccine, and it cuts both ways: play the Warden as hard as it can legally be played, and every mission you survive is one you actually earned.
:::

## Your First Mission {#sec-first-mission}

When you have read chapters 2 through 4 and skimmed the fight chapters, go straight to the first scenario of {{ref:ch-pineland-burning}}. It is built as a teaching mission: a small crew, a quiet start, and a decision about when the shooting starts that belongs to you. Its briefing lists the exact cards, tokens, and map tiles to bring to the table. Keep the quick reference at your elbow, play the Warden in good faith, and get everyone to the Exfil.

The valley is waiting to find out what you can reach.
